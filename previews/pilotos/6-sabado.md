# Piloto: Sábados Científicos

## ¿MITO O CIENCIA?

[MUSIC: intro]

ALEX [warm, welcoming]: Buenos días, and welcome to Sábados Científicos. Hoy el Chino descansa, and joining me at the mic is la experta del show.

ASHLEY [excited, a little nervous]: Hi, everyone! Okay, I've been waiting all week for this one.

ALEX [amused]: I know. You've been texting me diagrams.

ASHLEY [laughing]: Only like six. So, today's "¿Mito o Ciencia?": some people online still say the Earth is flat. And I wanted to show how anyone, with no satellites and no NASA, can prove it's round.

ALEX [interested]: Go ahead. Number one?

ASHLEY [enthusiastic, clear]: Eclipses. During a lunar eclipse, the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon, and that shadow is always round. Every eclipse, from every angle. A flat disc would sometimes cast a thin line, like a coin seen from the side. Only a sphere casts a round shadow every time. Aristotle used this argument more than two thousand years ago.

ALEX [nodding]: Mm-hmm. Geometry doing the work.

ASHLEY [delighted]: Exactly! Number two: ships. When a ship sails away, the bottom disappears first and the top of the mast disappears last. It's going over the curve.

ALEX [adding, engineer mode]: And you can do this one here in New Mexico with the night sky. Polaris, the North Star, sits about as high above the horizon as your latitude. Aquí en Albuquerque estamos como a treinta y cinco grados norte, so Polaris is about thirty-five degrees up. Go south to the equator and it sinks to the horizon. On a flat Earth, that wouldn't happen.

ASHLEY [impressed]: Okay, that one's so good. And my favorite, number three: Eratosthenes.

ALEX [smiling]: The classic.

ASHLEY [storytelling]: Around 240 BC, in Egypt, he noticed that at noon on a certain day, the Sun shone straight down a well in one city, Syene, with no shadow. But at the same time in Alexandria, farther north, a stick cast a shadow of about seven degrees. If the Earth were flat, the shadows would match. Using that angle and the distance between the cities, he estimated the size of the whole planet, and he got remarkably close to what we measure today, about forty thousand kilometers around.

ALEX [genuinely impressed]: With a stick, a well and a lot of patience. Veredicto, experta?

ASHLEY [proud]: ¡Mito! The Earth is round. Bueno... ¿cómo se dice? Redonda. Ay, remember que soy no sabo, Doc.

ALEX [warm, laughing]: Redonda, perfecto. Your Spanish is better than you think.

## PREGÚNTALE A DOC

ALEX [transitioning]: Now, Pregúntale a Doc. Today's question comes from right here in the studio.

ASHLEY [curious]: Yes! Why is the sky in New Mexico so blue? Like, when I visit family in other places, it never looks like this.

ALEX [explaining, clear]: Great question. First, why is any sky blue: sunlight has all the colors, and when it hits the air molecules, the blue light gets scattered much more than the red. That scattered blue comes at us from every direction. That's called Rayleigh scattering.

ASHLEY [nodding]: Okay, so the air is basically bouncing the blue around.

ALEX [continuing]: Exactly. Now, why New Mexico specifically: two things. Albuquerque sits at about fifty-three hundred feet, so there's less air and less haze above us. And our air is dry. Water droplets and dust scatter all colors, which makes the sky look whiter, más pálido. Less of that here, so the blue looks deeper.

ASHLEY [delighted]: So it's the altitude and the dry air. That's why the Sandias look so sharp.

ALEX [warm]: And why the sunsets are so good. Okay, experta, that's our show.

ASHLEY [cheerful]: Thanks for listening! Send us your questions, and see you next Saturday.

ALEX [closing]: Hasta el próximo sábado, Burque.
