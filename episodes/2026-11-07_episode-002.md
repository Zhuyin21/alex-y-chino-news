### Why the Sun pulled down NASA's Swift telescope

CHINO [excited]: Y cambiando de tema, ya llegamos al Taller. Doc, explícame bien. ¿Cómo se cae un telescopio que llevaba veintidós años allá arriba?

ALEX [clear, setting it up]: Primero, los hechos. NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory launched in 2004 to study gamma-ray bursts, the most powerful explosions in the universe. It was planned as a two-year mission. It lasted almost twenty-two. According to NASA, the U.S. Space Force confirmed it reentered on Wednesday, November 4th, at 7:39 a.m. Eastern, over North Sumatra, Indonesia.

CHINO [impressed]: ¡Veintidós años de garantía, Doc! Ni mi troca aguanta tanto.

ALEX [laughing, then explaining]: Ha. And it earned it. Per Spaceflight Now, Swift caught more than two thousand gamma-ray bursts, studied more than fourteen hundred supernovas, and its data shows up in about seven thousand scientific papers. In 2017, when gravitational-wave detectors heard two neutron stars crash together, Swift saw surprisingly bright ultraviolet light from the wreckage.

CHINO [curious]: Okay, Doc, pero ¿eso cómo funciona? ¿Por qué se cae? Yo pensaba que en el espacio no hay aire.

ALEX [explaining, engineer]: Almost no air, and that "almost" is the whole story. At Swift's altitude, the air is incredibly thin, but it's not zero. A satellite is moving around seventeen thousand miles an hour, so it's slamming into those few air molecules over and over. Every collision steals a tiny bit of speed. Lose speed, and you drop a little lower. Swift had no engine, so it couldn't push back up.

CHINO [thinking]: Como cuando vas en la bici sin pedalear. Poquito a poquito se va frenando, hasta que ni modo, te bajas.

ALEX [pleased]: Eso, that's exactly it. Now here's where the Sun comes in. Spaceflight Now reports NASA said solar activity heated and expanded the upper atmosphere, and that caused more drag than expected. NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center explains the mechanism: when the Sun dumps extra energy into the atmosphere, the air puffs up, and denser air from lower down rises to satellite altitudes.

CHINO [skeptical]: ¿Se infla? ¿Como una tortilla en el comal?

ALEX [delighted]: ¡Eee, Chino! That's a better analogy than mine. Yes. You heat the tortilla, the air inside expands, and it puffs up. Heat the upper atmosphere, it expands, and suddenly a satellite that was flying through almost nothing is flying through something thicker. And it snowballs: more drag, it drops lower, the air down there is denser, so even more drag. Per NOAA, satellites in low orbit might need a boost about four times a year when the Sun is quiet, but at the peak of the eleven-year solar cycle, they may need one every two to three weeks.

CHINO [surprised]: ¡No manches, Doc! De cuatro veces al año a cada dos semanas. Qué caray.

ALEX [serious, steady]: And it's not new. NOAA notes that during a big solar storm in March 1989, NASA's Solar Maximum Mission satellite reportedly "dropped as if it hit a brick wall."

CHINO [curious]: Pero sí intentaron rescatarlo, ¿verdad? Yo vi algo de un robot.

ALEX [explaining]: They did, and it's a good engineering story. Per Spaceflight Now, NASA paid a company called Katalyst Space thirty million dollars in 2025 to send a robotic tug called LINK. The plan was to grab Swift and push it up to roughly three hundred seventy miles. LINK launched July 3rd. But about a week into the mission, a power switch tied to its reaction wheels failed. Reaction wheels are spinning wheels a spacecraft uses to point itself. Without them, LINK lost control of its orientation, and by late July it was tumbling once every forty seconds.

CHINO [wincing]: Ay, no. Dando vueltas como trompo. Pobre robotito.

ALEX [measured]: NASA called off the capture in August. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said, quote, "This is not the outcome we were working toward, but it does not change why this mission was worth attempting." Meanwhile, the Penn State team that ran Swift paused most of the science and tilted the spacecraft to cut down its drag, to buy time. Swift made its last observation October 20th, then went into safe mode at about one hundred seventy-one miles up.

CHINO [concerned]: ¿Y nos debimos preocupar? ¿Le pudo caer algo a alguien?

ALEX [calm, reassuring]: Spaceflight Now reports the spacecraft weighed about 1.6 tons, and NASA put the risk of harm to anyone on Earth at about one in 2,100. NASA expected most of it to burn up, though some parts may have survived. Most of the planet under that path is ocean.

CHINO [thoughtful]: Okay, Doc. ¿Y ahora qué sigue? ¿Qué debemos estar viendo?

ALEX [looking ahead]: Three things. First, NASA hasn't named a replacement for Swift, so astronomers lose a fast responder that could swing around and catch a burst in minutes. Second, Hubble. Spaceflight Now reports Hubble also has no engine, NASA expects it to reenter around 2033 or 2034, and on October 29th NASA asked companies how they might boost or service it, with a mission needed by 2032. Third, watch whether satellite rescues like LINK get a second shot. This one failed, but the idea of servicing old spacecraft instead of letting them fall isn't going away. Ahí te watcho on that one.

CHINO [warm]: Pues descanse en paz, Swift. Veintidós años chambeando sin vacaciones. Eso sí es ser trabajador.

### Sandia: aiming at a lumpy asteroid

CHINO [proud]: Closer to home, y de veras bien cerquita, en Sandia. Doc, ¿es cierto que están estudiando cómo desviar un asteroide con una bomba nuclear?

ALEX [clear]: Es cierto, pero todo es simulación en computadora. Sandia physicist Nathan Moore led a study published in the journal Icarus. The idea: if a dangerous asteroid were coming, a nuclear detonation near it would blast one side with X-rays. That vaporizes a thin layer of the surface, and the vapor shooting off pushes the asteroid the other way, like a little rocket engine made of rock.

CHINO [curious]: ¿Y qué descubrieron?

ALEX [explaining]: That aim matters a lot more than people assumed. Older models often treated asteroids like smooth balls. Moore's team used real 3D shapes from spacecraft flybys of three asteroids: Golevka, Bennu and Itokawa. For one simulated asteroid, the push was 3.9 millimeters per second from one side and only 0.8 from another. That's almost five times different. For another, the change in its spin varied by a factor of seventeen.

CHINO [laughing]: ¡Es como la piñata, Doc! Si le pegas chueco, nomás da vueltas y no se rompe.

ALEX [amused]: Ha, pretty much. Hit it off-center and you get a lot of spin instead of push. Moore said, quote, "We expected aim to matter. What surprised us was how much." Per Sandia and The Register, the team combined two Sandia codes, called ALEGRA and SPARTA, and made the calculation more than sixteen hundred times faster, so they could run many more scenarios.

CHINO [thoughtful]: ¿Y tiene sus limitaciones, o ya estamos listos pa'l asteroide?

ALEX [measured]: Limitations, sí. Sandia says the model predicts surface breakup, not whether the whole asteroid shatters. And you need accurate shape data from every side, which you might not have in time. A few millimeters per second sounds tiny, but applied years ahead of time, a small, well-aimed push can add up to a miss. Puro Burque, cuidando el planeta.

## ¿MITO O CIENCIA?

[MUSIC: sting]

### Does the supermoon cause earthquakes?

CHINO [playful]: Switching gears, a mi segmento favorito: ¿Mito o Ciencia? Ahí les va el WhatsApp de mi tía, palabra por palabra: "Con la superluna del 24 va a temblar fuerte. Junten agua." Doc, ¿le hago caso o no?

ALEX [warm, respectful]: Your tía sent it because she cares, so let's take it seriously. There's a grain of real science here. The Moon and Sun don't just pull on the oceans. They also stretch the solid Earth a tiny bit, and that adds stress to faults. That stress is biggest around new and full moons.

CHINO [surprised]: ¿A poco? Entonces mi tía tiene razón.

ALEX [explaining]: Pues, a little. A 2016 study in Nature Geoscience by Satoshi Ide's team at the University of Tokyo looked at earthquake records from Japan, California and the whole world. Among more than ten thousand quakes around magnitude 5.5, the ones that started during high tidal stress were somewhat more likely to grow into big ones. But Ide himself told Nature the effect is, quote, "too small to take some actions."

CHINO [curious]: ¿Y alguien revisó si los terremotos grandes caen en luna llena?

ALEX [clear]: Yes. In 2018, USGS seismologist Susan Hough looked at more than two hundred magnitude-8-or-bigger earthquakes going back four centuries, published in Seismological Research Letters. Her question was whether they happen on preferred days of the lunar cycle. The paper's one-word summary: "No." Sin rodeos.

CHINO [ready]: Okay, Doc. ¿El veredicto?

ALEX [firm, kind]: Mito. Tides can give a fault a very small nudge, but nobody can predict an earthquake from the moon, supermoon or not. That said, tell your tía that keeping water and an emergency kit at home is a good idea any day of the year.

CHINO [laughing, warm]: Le voy a decir que el Doc dice que sí junte agua, pero que no se desvele esperando el temblor.

## PREGÚNTALE A DOC

[MUSIC: sting]

### Do solar panels work when it's cloudy?

CHINO [warm]: Y ahora, Pregúntale a Doc. Esta pregunta nos la mandó Chuy por GitHub: "¿Producen energía los paneles solares cuando está nublado? ¿Cuánta, y por qué?"

ALEX [explaining, engineer]: Great question, and the short answer is yes, less, but not zero. Here's why. The Department of Energy splits sunlight into two kinds. Direct beam is the straight shot from the Sun, the light that makes sharp shadows. Diffuse light is the sunlight that got scattered by air, dust and clouds, the glow you see on an overcast day. DOE says the atmosphere can cut the direct beam by about ten percent on a clear, dry day and by a hundred percent on a thick, cloudy day.

CHINO [curious]: ¿Y los paneles agarran esa luz que rebota?

ALEX [clear]: They do. A solar cell doesn't care whether light came straight from the Sun or bounced off a cloud. It just gets fewer photons. How much less? One study of a fifty-megawatt solar plant in Ghana, published in the journal Electricity, found the cloudiest day of each month made anywhere from about seventeen percent to ninety-four percent less energy than the sunniest day. Thin clouds, small hit. Heavy, dark overcast, you lose most of it.

CHINO [surprised]: Pues sí baja bastante, Doc.

ALEX [smiling]: It does. But here's a fun twist. Sometimes clouds give you more. When sunlight hits the bright edge of a cloud, it can reflect extra light down onto you. A 2020 study measured peaks of 1,466 watts per square meter in San Diego. That's more than the roughly 1,360 watts per square meter that NOAA says arrives at the top of the atmosphere. Engineers have to size equipment for those spikes.

CHINO [delighted]: ¡Órale! ¿Y aquí en Burque?

ALEX [warm]: Mira, one cloudy day isn't a big deal. What matters for your panels is the total over the whole year, not one gray afternoon. And remember those space station arrays from the top of the show? Allá arriba no hay nubes. Thanks for the question, Chuy.
