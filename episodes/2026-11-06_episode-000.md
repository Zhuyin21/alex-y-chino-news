# Alex y Chino: Episode 0 (Pilot)

## COLD OPEN (~1 min)

CHINO [upbeat, morning energy]: ¡Buenos días, Burque! It's Friday, November sixth. Soy Chino.

ALEX [dry, amused]: And I'm Dr. Alex. And Chino, I think this is the first Friday in two years where nobody's sending us a campaign text.

CHINO [laughing]: Ay, gracias a Dios. My phone finally stopped vibrating. My abuela thought it was possessed, Doc.

ALEX [matter-of-fact]: Weather first. Today starts dry, but a low-pressure system pulls moisture in. Scattered showers move across western, central and southern New Mexico, and the metro has a rain chance after about 4 pm. Amounts should be light and the flood risk is very low. There's snow up high. Sunday turns dry and sunny again, and next week runs warmer than average.

CHINO [warm]: So, umbrella for the drive home, sunglasses for Sunday. Órale. Big show today. We've got a new governor, a sheriff's office scandal getting deeper, and a midterm that, eh, did not go quietly. ¡Vámonos!

## LOCAL: ALBUQUERQUE + BERNALILLO COUNTY (~9 min)

### 1. Governor-elect Haaland's first day

CHINO [proud, a little emotional]: Okay, story number one, and this one's historic. Deb Haaland won the governor's race Tuesday night. She beat Republican Gregg Hull, the former longtime mayor of Rio Rancho. She's from Laguna Pueblo, and she's the first Native American woman elected governor of any state. Ever.

ALEX [steady]: And the next morning she went straight to Barelas Coffee House with Lieutenant Governor-elect Stephanie Garcia Richard. Shook hands, took photos, and laid out priorities: affordability, education, public safety, finding a new leader for CYFD, and working with the Legislature so New Mexicans don't lose health coverage.

CHINO [teasing]: Barelas Coffee House! That's the move, Doc. Every politician goes there. If you win New Mexico and you don't go eat chile at Barelas, does it even count?

ALEX [dry]: I'd want a larger sample size before I commit to that theory.

CHINO [laughing]: Ya empezó con el sample size.

ALEX [more serious, analytical]: Here's the part that caught my eye. She said on day one she wants a moratorium on large-scale data centers, with the Legislature passing guardrails. Her stated reasons were protecting water, land and air, and keeping New Mexicans' energy bills from going up.

CHINO [curious]: Okay, explain this one for me. Why does a building full of computers care about our water?

ALEX [explaining, accessible]: Big data centers produce a ton of heat, and a lot of them cool with water. They also pull huge amounts of electricity from the grid. How much water depends a lot on the design, and some newer facilities use far less. But in a state with a megadrought, it's a fair question. The other side has a real argument too: these projects bring construction jobs and tax base, and a moratorium can push that investment to Arizona or Texas. So the evidence-based answer is: it depends on the rules. We'll see what guardrails actually look like.

CHINO [nodding]: Okay. And it's not just us. Hold on to that, because it comes back in the national segment.

ALEX [neutral]: She also launched a transition website, HaalandTransition.com, where people can apply to work in the administration. She takes office in January.

### 2. The rest of your ballot

CHINO [quick, upbeat]: Rapid-fire on the other results, porque hubo un montón. First: Congressman Gabe Vasquez won reelection in the Second District. That's the swing seat that covers southern New Mexico and reaches up into the South Valley and the West Side. He beat Republican Greg Cunningham, a retired APD detective.

ALEX [interested]: And this one's actually new for New Mexico. Voters approved Constitutional Amendment 4, which allows salaries for state legislators. Right now New Mexico is the only state in the country where legislators don't get a salary.

CHINO [shocked]: Wait, wait. They make zero?

ALEX [matter-of-fact]: No salary. They get per diem and mileage. Under the amendment, the salary is tied to the state's median household income, roughly sixty-eight thousand dollars. It starts in 2029, and covering all 112 legislators costs about 7.6 million a year.

CHINO [thoughtful]: Okay, I kind of like it. If it's unpaid, who can even run? Retired people and rich people. My tío works two jobs. He's never going to Santa Fe for sixty days for free.

ALEX [fair]: That's the main argument supporters made: more regular people can afford to serve. The critics weren't all against pay in principle. The Rio Grande Foundation said this number is too high, higher than neighboring states, and the state GOP opposed the amendment. And for perspective, voters crushed a pay measure back in 1990, roughly 75 to 25. That's a big shift in public opinion.

CHINO [quick]: Last one. Albuquerque voters approved making the head of Albuquerque Community Safety, ACS, a chief-level position, the same rank as the police and fire chiefs. About seventy percent voted yes. The mayor has to nominate a chief by February 15th, and City Council confirms.

### 3. The DWI scandal reaches deeper into BCSO

ALEX [serious]: Story three, and it's not a light one. A federal grand jury indicted former Bernalillo County sheriff's deputy Paul Jessen Junior in the DWI corruption scheme. You know, the one where officers allegedly skipped court so drunk-driving cases got thrown out.

CHINO [frustrated]: Otra vez. Este pinche escándalo nunca se acaba.

ALEX [precise]: Here's what prosecutors allege. From March 2018 to January 2024, Jessen worked with defense attorney Thomas Clear the Third and paralegal Ricardo Mendez. He skipped required court settings for Clear's DWI clients, knowing his absence would let Clear get the cases dismissed. The indictment says he was uncomfortable taking cash, so he took gifts instead: a Pit Boss grill and smoker, a Sharp big-screen TV, Craftsman tools and a toolbox, free legal help, and hotel rooms.

CHINO [incredulous, dry]: A Pit Boss? Allegedly. Doc, that's not a bribe. That's a carne asada starter kit.

ALEX [half-smile, then serious]: Allegedly. He hasn't been convicted on these charges. But context matters. Earlier this year he was convicted in a separate case of obstructing a DEA investigation and lying to the FBI. BCSO fired him in September. If he's convicted here, he faces up to 20 years.

ALEX [pointed]: Two things worth noting. First, the indictment mentions a "senior BCSO deputy" as a co-conspirator, and prosecutors haven't said who that is. Second, the damage is real: in 2024 alone, the DA's office dismissed more than two hundred DWI cases because of the credibility problems with officers under investigation. Some of those drivers were probably dangerous, and they're back on the road.

CHINO [serious]: Yeah. Corrupt cops aside, that's the part that scares me. People who drove drunk on our roads.

### 4. Flooding emergency in Sandoval County

CHINO [concerned]: Story four, close to home. Sandoval County declared a disaster emergency after severe flooding. Roads washed out, water systems damaged, homes and businesses at risk. The hardest-hit areas include the Ojo Encino, Torreon and Counselor chapters of the Navajo Nation.

ALEX [steady]: An emergency declaration matters because it opens the door to state and potentially federal help with repairs. If you've got family out that way, check on them. Rural water systems take a while to come back.

### 5. River of Lights, and Lotaburger

CHINO [excited again]: Okay, something happy to close local. River of Lights is back for its 29th year at the BioPark. It opens Saturday, November 28th, with fifty new sculptures, and some of them celebrate the Route 66 centennial. And this is the best part, Doc: Blake's Lotaburger is the sponsor for the first time.

ALEX [deadpan]: You're more excited about the sponsor than the lights.

CHINO [laughing]: Hey, a green chile cheeseburger and lights? That's the perfect date. Tickets go on sale today at 10 am, so if you want a good weekend slot, set a reminder.

## UNITED STATES (~7 min)

### 1. Democrats take Congress

ALEX [analytical, measured]: National. The big one: Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate on Tuesday. As of Thursday morning, CNBC's count had Democrats at 250 or more House seats, and a few races are still being counted. One election analyst on Euronews called it the largest House majority in decades. We'll give you final numbers once they're certified.

CHINO [energetic]: Two-fifty! Doc, that's not a wave. That's a tsunami. Bring the boogie board.

ALEX [dry, scientific]: Some perspective, though. Historically, the president's party loses House seats in almost every midterm since World War II. The only exceptions were 1998 and 2002. So a loss was the expected direction. It's the size that's unusual.

CHINO [curious]: And why was it so big?

ALEX [explaining]: According to NPR's reporting, voter frustration over the cost of living was a top driver. Democrats ran on lowering costs and corruption. And practically, a House majority means subpoena power, which means investigations. Market analysts are already pointing to the debt ceiling fight in 2027 as the big flashpoint.

CHINO [wry]: So, basically, everybody's broke and mad. Relatable.

### 2. The Republican fallout

CHINO [lively]: On the other side there's a lot of finger-pointing. NPR reports House Speaker Mike Johnson won't stay on as the top House Republican in the next Congress. Vice President JD Vance openly blamed the war with Iran for the losses, and gas prices hit people right in the wallet.

ALEX [neutral, careful]: And President Trump took a conciliatory tone with CNBC, saying he can work with Democrats on "many more things than people understand." He also brushed off talk of impeachment, saying it would backfire on Democrats. To be fair to both sides: Republicans argue it was one bad cycle driven by a war and fuel prices, not a rejection of their whole agenda. Democrats argue it was a referendum on affordability and corruption. The exit data will tell us more over the next few weeks.

### 3. Texas turned upside down

CHINO [amazed]: Doc, our neighbors! Texas! Democrat James Talarico won the Senate race against Ken Paxton. And this is según the market analysts at XTB: Elon Musk spent a record two hundred twenty-four million dollars backing Paxton. No mames, wey, ¡doscientos veinticuatro millones! And he still lost.

ALEX [dry]: That's an expensive way to learn money doesn't guarantee votes. Texas also elected Democrat Gina Hinojosa as governor. She's promised tougher rules for data centers.

CHINO [snapping fingers]: ¡Ahí está! The data centers again. Haaland here, Hinojosa there.

### 4. Wall Street's AI hangover

ALEX [analytical]: Which brings us to markets. US stocks fell Thursday, led by a selloff in AI stocks, per Al Jazeera. AI stocks were already sliding before the election. One estimate says they've lost roughly five trillion dollars in value since early October. Goldman Sachs estimates that about forty-five percent of planned US data center capacity is in states that could tighten regulation, but it expects any pauses to be short-lived.

CHINO [practical]: Okay, translate for the people with a 401(k). Should I panic?

ALEX [calm]: This isn't financial advice. But short-term swings around elections are normal, and on average the market has done well in the months after a midterm. The real story is that "build data centers everywhere, as fast as possible" now faces political resistance in two big Southwest states. Investors are repricing that.

### 5. Shake-up at the Justice Department

CHINO [quick]: Last national story. Harmeet Dhillon, the head of the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division, is out after about eighteen months. NPR called it a tumultuous tenure. Al Jazeera reports she resigned.

ALEX [neutral]: That division enforces voting rights, police misconduct and discrimination laws. So whoever replaces her matters, especially with a new Congress that wants to hold hearings. No replacement has been named yet.

## WORLD (~7 min)

### 1. Ukraine: a deadly strike, and talk of talks

ALEX [somber, no humor]: World. On Monday, Russia bombed a school being used to hand out humanitarian aid in Komyshuvakha, in Ukraine's Zaporizhzhia region. According to Ukrainian officials, after the first strike, nine more bombs hit the shelter where people were taking cover. Early reports put the death toll at 24 to 26. Reuters now reports Ukrainian police say it's reached 42.

CHINO [quiet]: People lining up for food. No hay palabras.

ALEX [steady]: On the diplomatic side, Turkey's President Erdogan says he wants to discuss ending the war with Putin, after talks with Zelensky. Italy is finalizing a new military aid package for Ukraine. And Zelensky had said trilateral talks with the US and Russia in Abu Dhabi could happen as early as November 10th. We'll see if that holds.

### 2. Iran war: shipping still under fire

CHINO [serious]: Story two. The war with Iran keeps hitting shipping in the Gulf. The British military says a tanker was hit by projectiles off the coast of Qatar this week. And remember, this is why your gas is expensive, compa.

ALEX [analytical]: Right, it connects straight back to the midterms. NPR reports a new Democratic majority could pressure the president to end the war, and foreign governments are watching closely. Meanwhile, Al Jazeera reports President Trump said the US may have to "destroy" the rest of Iran. So the signals from Washington are mixed: conciliatory on domestic politics, hawkish on Iran. Markets are betting on diplomacy. The White House hasn't committed either way.

### 3. Hong Kong: Joshua Wong gets 10 more years

ALEX [neutral, serious]: Hong Kong. NPR reports pro-democracy activist Joshua Wong was sentenced to 10 years in prison. It's his second conviction under the national security law Beijing imposed in 2020. Prosecutors said he conspired with foreign forces, including lobbying for a US law that allowed sanctions on Hong Kong and China before the law took effect. Ten years is the top of the standard sentencing range.

CHINO [disbelief]: He was a teenager when this all started, right?

ALEX [factual]: He became famous as a teenage student leader. He's 29 now and had already spent years in prison. Beijing and the Hong Kong government argue the law restored stability after the 2019 protests. Critics, including Western governments and human rights groups, say it's being used to crush peaceful dissent.

### 4. Bomb hoaxes on European flights

CHINO [curious]: Story four, kind of a weird one. Bomb threats against flights all over Europe. Czech police say four planes headed to Prague got threats. The BBC counted seven flights from several countries.

ALEX [careful]: All hoaxes so far, nothing found. The EU's transport chief says it's too early to say whether the threats are connected. Czech intelligence has previously blamed Russia for a separate wave of school bomb threats, but nobody has officially linked this week's flight threats to anyone. So: concerning, unproven.

CHINO [relieved]: Okay, good. No bombs. Still, imagine being on that plane. Hell no, wey. I'd need a tequila. Or three.

### 5. New Zealand votes Saturday

ALEX [lighter, curious]: And something to watch this weekend: New Zealand holds its general election on Saturday. Recent polls show the two biggest parties within a couple of points of each other, so it really could go either way.

CHINO [joking]: Doc, I'll be honest: everything I know about New Zealand is from Lord of the Rings.

ALEX [deadpan]: That's roughly ninety percent of Americans. We'll have results Monday.
